
I believe it is time for a serious evaluation of how foundational subjects, particularly **Mathematics**, are being taught at Bangladesh University of Engineering and Technology (BUET), my alma mater and the country's premier engineering institution.

During my undergraduate studies, I completed courses in **Calculus, Linear Algebra, and Probability & Statistics**. Looking back, despite attending hundreds of lectures and achieving good grades, I feel that I developed far less conceptual understanding than I should have.

Like many of my classmates, I learned to solve familiar problem types, practiced previous years' questions, and performed well in examinations. However, when I entered research, I quickly realized that mathematics is not just another course. **It is the language of engineering.**

Whether in Electrical Engineering, Power Systems, or any technical field, meaningful research demands mathematical intuition and problem-solving ability—not simply familiarity with standard procedures. My grades reflected my exam performance, but not necessarily genuine understanding.

It was only after watching lectures such as MIT OpenCourseWare's Linear Algebra course that I realized how engaging and intuitive mathematics could be.

I do not expect every instructor to teach at that level, and I fully acknowledge my own responsibility to learn beyond the classroom. Nevertheless, one of the primary responsibilities of teaching should be to **cultivate curiosity and develop intuition**, rather than inadvertently encouraging students to rely on memorization.

If this is the experience at BUET, I cannot help but wonder what the situation is like at many other universities across the country.

I sincerely hope the university reflects on how foundational subjects are taught. Engineering education should aim to develop **analytical thinkers**, not simply graduates who perform well in examinations.

If necessary, BUET should recruit educators—whether from within Bangladesh or abroad—who not only possess deep subject knowledge but also understand how to inspire genuine learning.

Otherwise, we risk producing engineers with impressive transcripts but weak fundamentals.

**And that is a problem that ultimately affects the quality of engineering itself.**
